Three in four homes now have a pet

Ownership dipped slightly before 2020, then jumped during the pandemic years. It kept climbing afterwards, so this was not just a lockdown fad.

In 2016, 62% of households had a pet. The figure reached 73% by 2025. This is the national picture before we look at which animals Australians keep.

Australia's favourite companions

Dogs are the most widely owned pet type, found in 49% of Australian households in 2025. But when we count individual animals, fish come first: there are about 12.1 million pet fish compared with 7.4 million dogs.

That's because fish-owning households keep an average of 10 fish, while dog-owning households average 1.4 dogs. Household ownership rates and the number of individual pets answer different questions.

How to read the treemap: each rectangle's area represents the estimated number of individual animals of that type, not the share of households owning them. Hover for the estimated count.

Who owns pets? Young people, families and mortgage holders

Nine in ten students and 18 to 24 year olds have a pet. So do 85% of families with children and of people paying off a mortgage.

Pets are least common among retirees, over-60s and apartment dwellers. Rural Australians break the usual pattern: 81% have a pet even though rural incomes are lower. Money is not the whole story either, since 74% of people in financial difficulty own a pet, against 73% of those doing well.

How to read the chart: each bar shows the share of that group with a pet. The dashed line is the 73% national average; green bars are above it and orange bars below it. † The report gives these groups' share of non-owners, so we show 100% minus that number.

Why do Australians get a pet?

Companionship comes first for dogs, cats, birds and small mammals. Fish are the exception: owners get them to relax.

Mental health is among the top four reasons for every kind of pet. For small mammals it has jumped from 8% of owners in 2022 to 29% in 2025.

How to read the rose: each wedge is one reason, and the wedge's area shows the share of owners who gave it. Only each pet's top reasons are shown.

Cats are mostly adopted. Dogs are mostly bought.

Three in four cats came from a shelter, the street or someone they knew. For dogs it is fewer than half. A dog is also dearer: owners who paid spent $1,419 on average, against $419 for a cat.

Attitudes are warming. About half of owners say they would probably or only adopt next time, and 62% agree there is no need to buy when so many shelter animals need homes.

Among owners who did not adopt, the top reason for not considering it was wanting a particular breed (38% for both cats and dogs).

An average dog-owning home spends $2,520 a year. A cat-owning home spends $1,656. Food is the biggest bill for almost every pet.

Reptile owners are the exception: vet bills are their biggest cost.

Together, Australians now spend about a third more on pets than in 2022, and most of that growth comes from dogs.

How to read the table: darker plum means more money. Grey cells mean the survey did not ask about that item for that pet.

Dogs see the vet more. Cats are desexed more.

Dogs are ahead on vet visits, vaccination and microchipping. Cats are ahead on desexing, which is also more likely to be included in the price of a cat.

Cost is part of the story: 22% of cat owners and 30% of dog owners who visited the vet less often said they could not afford it.

Pets are family, and owners act like it

Almost every pet-parenting habit grew since 2022. Six in ten owners talk to their pets as if they understand. One in seven has installed a camera to check on them.

Love is growing, and so is the strain

Owning a pet is getting harder, mostly because of money. Ongoing costs are now owners' biggest difficulty, and a growing number of owners are cutting back on their pet's health care.

Cost is also the top reason people who want a pet do not have one: 26% name it, up from 21% in 2022.

Source: AMA, Pets in Australia 2025, chapters 5 and 7. Cost-cutting figures are for dog and cat owners.
